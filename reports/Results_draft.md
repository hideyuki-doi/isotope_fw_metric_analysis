## Freshwater food webs thin out toward the poles

We analysed 606 freshwater food webs with at least 10 isotopically characterised taxa, spanning lakes (lentic) and rivers and streams (lotic) from the tropics to ~70° latitude (Fig. 1a). Network connectance, the proportion of taxon pairs that are linked in isotope space, declined with absolute latitude (Fig. 1b; mixed model with Region/Source random effects, P < 0.001), as did the number of taxa per web (Fig. 1c; P = 0.003). Connectance increased with taxon number (Fig. 1d; P < 0.001), so any latitudinal pattern in network structure must be separated from the effect of diversity.

## Climate effects on network structure are consistent across drivers and ecosystems

Moving poleward consistently reduced connectance, nestedness and mean link strength (Fig. 2). Standardised effects on connectance were −0.29 for latitude, −0.27 for cooler temperature and −0.31 for higher temperature seasonality (all FDR q < 0.01; pooled ecosystems); effects on nestedness (z-NODF, −0.20 to −0.27) and mean link strength (−0.25 to −0.29) were of similar size. Mean closeness declined more weakly and was significant only for latitude. For connectance and link strength the direction and significance were the same in lentic and lotic webs [CHECK per-ecosystem estimates in Table S1].

Because the number of individuals analysed per web varies widely (median 70, interquartile range 39–135, range 1–998; 7% of webs lacked sample sizes) and increases with taxon number (r = 0.30) but not with latitude (r = 0.04), we tested whether sampling effort could explain these patterns. Adding log10 individuals as a covariate left the effects essentially unchanged (connectance −0.28 for latitude, −0.26 for temperature, −0.30 for seasonality; all q < 0.01; Fig. S5). Restricting the analysis to the 282 webs with at least the median number of individuals strengthened the effects (connectance −0.38, −0.40 and −0.32 for latitude, temperature and seasonality respectively; all q < 0.001). The temperature–connectance relationship was also robust to the choice of the global link threshold, which we varied from the 30th to the 80th percentile of pairwise similarity (standardised effect 0.20–0.28, all P < 0.005; Fig. S1).

## Apparent modularity gradients disappear after null-model correction

Raw modularity (Q) was higher toward the poles (latitude +0.22, cooler temperature +0.23, seasonality +0.28; q < 0.01), which would naively suggest that cold, seasonal food webs are more compartmentalised. However, Q depends strongly on network size and link density. After standardising Q against a null model that permutes pairwise similarities (z-Q), modularity showed no relationship with any climate variable (latitude −0.02, q = 0.72; temperature, q = 0.32; seasonality, q = 0.72), and this remained true after adjusting for sampling effort (latitude q = 0.66; temperature q = 0.22; seasonality q = 0.91). The raw modularity gradient therefore reflects network size and density rather than a change in compartmentalisation per se.

## Connectance retains a direct climate effect; nestedness and link strength act mainly through diversity

To separate direct climate effects from those mediated by diversity, we fitted piecewise structural equation models in which climate affects taxon richness, richness affects the spread of basal resources in isotope space (prey niche area), and all three influence network structure, with sampling effort as a covariate in every equation (n = 474 webs; Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Sampling effort was the strongest predictor of observed richness (standardised path 0.74), and latitude still reduced richness after accounting for it (−0.20, P = 0.046). Richness, in turn, increased prey niche area (0.45) and strongly increased connectance (0.32), nestedness (0.67) and link strength (0.70); prey niche area increased connectance (0.29) and link strength (0.14) but not nestedness (−0.02).

For connectance, latitude had a significant direct effect (−0.14, P = 0.027) that accounted for about 57% of the total effect (−0.24); the remainder was transmitted through richness (−0.06) and, to a lesser extent, prey niche area (−0.04). Cooler temperature and higher seasonality showed the same pattern (direct effects −0.12 and −0.15, P = 0.048 and 0.022). In contrast, nestedness was affected mainly through richness (−0.14 of a total −0.20, about 69%), and its direct climate effect was not significant for any driver (P = 0.07–0.27). Link strength was intermediate: the direct latitude effect was significant (−0.10, P = 0.037; about 40% of the total, −0.26) but direct effects of temperature and seasonality were not (P = 0.20 and 0.054).

Cluster-bootstrap mediation analysis, resampling whole studies, supported these decompositions (Fig. S2). Richness-mediated indirect effects excluded zero for connectance, nestedness and link strength for all three climate drivers (for latitude: connectance −0.11, 95% CI −0.18 to −0.03; nestedness −0.17, −0.29 to −0.05; link strength −0.20, −0.36 to −0.06). Indirect effects through prey niche area were smaller and excluded zero for temperature and seasonality but not for latitude. Including sampling effort reduced the richness-mediated paths by roughly one third relative to the model without it (Fig. S6) while leaving direct effects unchanged, indicating that part of the apparent diversity effect reflects sampling intensity but that the direct effect on connectance does not.

The null-corrected modularity metric showed a distinctive signature. Within the SEM, z-Q increased strongly with richness (standardised path 0.69, P < 0.001), so poleward declines in richness lowered z-Q indirectly (latitude −0.14; seasonality −0.11; cooler temperature −0.22). These indirect effects were offset by direct effects of the opposite sign, which were positive toward the poles (latitude +0.14, P = 0.052; seasonality +0.17, P = 0.011; cooler temperature +0.10, P = 0.12), leaving near-zero total effects (latitude +0.02; seasonality +0.09; cooler temperature −0.07). Raw modularity (Q), in contrast, was unrelated to richness once prey niche area was accounted for (P > 0.8) and showed significant direct poleward increases (latitude +0.16, P = 0.035; seasonality +0.23, P < 0.001; cooler temperature +0.18, P = 0.012). The absence of a net z-Q gradient therefore reflects cancellation between a diversity-mediated decline and a direct increase in compartmentalisation, rather than a lack of climate dependence (Table S2).

## Other candidate drivers and interactions

Among other candidate drivers (Fig. S3), taxon richness and the number of prey taxa were the strongest correlates of network structure, whereas lake area, fish omnivory indices and fish body-size traits showed weaker and less consistent associations; we therefore treat the body-size and omnivory results as exploratory. Neither temperature × seasonality interactions (minimum q = 0.38) nor body size × temperature interactions (minimum q = 0.07) were significant after false discovery rate correction (Fig. S4).
